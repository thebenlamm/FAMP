## 3. `iroh` — REACH-03 weighing, verified

- **Version:** `iroh` 1.0.3, published to crates.io **2026-07-20** (confirmed via crates.io API, live fetch). The 1.0 line itself launched **2026-06-15** (confirmed via n0's own blog, `iroh.computer/blog/v1`).
- **n0's public relay servers:** free, but **explicitly scoped to development and testing only**, and throughput is rate-limited "to prevent abuse" — n0's own FAQ states production workloads are expected to use either a self-hosted relay or n0's paid, managed "Iroh Services" dedicated relay tier (which advertises an uptime SLA; **no price for that managed tier was found on any page fetched during this research pass** — see COULD NOT VERIFY).
- **Self-hosting a relay:** documented and described as straightforward — n0's FAQ states you need "a server with a public IP and a DNS name pointing to it," with automatic ACME-based TLS built into the relay binary, which is itself open source and stateless ("they route encrypted packets but store nothing"). This is comparable in operational weight to standing up the self-hosted relay option already in STACK.md — i.e., choosing iroh doesn't obviously buy you out of running infrastructure, it mostly buys you out of writing the hole-punch code.
- **Direct-vs-relayed rate:** n0's own documentation states roughly **9 out of 10** connections end up fully direct once the relay assists the initial hole-punch, with the relay falling back to full relay-mode traffic only for the remaining ~10%.
- **Concrete answer to "does iroh give you relays for free":** **Partially, and only for dev/test.** For a real two-party production deployment, the honest framing is: either (a) self-host your own iroh relay (comparable ops burden to Option A's self-hosted axum relay, just running different binary/protocol), or (b) pay n0 for a managed dedicated relay (price unverified — ask n0 directly before assuming a number), or (c) accept that the free public relays are rate-limited and not intended for this use, which is a real risk for a production dependency. This confirms the STACK.md framing that iroh's cost is architectural (a transport swap), not "free relay infra with no catch" — the catch is real and vendor-documented, not merely inferred.

## 4. Symmetric-NAT / CGNAT prevalence — honest confidence assessment

**The 15–30% figure in REQUIREMENTS.md's REACH-06 (and echoed in earlier STACK.md drafts) could not be re-verified against a current, primary 2025/2026 measurement study.** What was found:

- The most concrete, citable, methodologically-primary source located is **Richter et al., "A Multi-perspective Analysis of Carrier-Grade NAT Deployment," ACM Internet Measurement Conference (IMC) 2016** (confirmed via dblp and ACM DL: `conf/imc/RichterWVABFKWP16`, arXiv:1605.05606). It reports: **~11% of non-cellular CGN Autonomous Systems have symmetric mapping as their most permissive NAT type**; for **cellular** networks, a **bimodal distribution**: roughly **40% symmetric-NAT-dominant, ~20% full-cone-dominant** among the ASes studied.
- **This paper is from 2016 — roughly a decade stale relative to today's date (2026-07-30).** Per the research constraints, this is flagged explicitly: mobile network architecture, NAT64/CGNAT deployment patterns, and IPv6 rollout have all shifted materially since 2016, and no equivalently rigorous, dated-2024/2025/2026 replication was located in this pass.
- A newer paper was found — **arXiv:2311.04658, "Mass Adoption of NATs: Survey and experiments on carrier-grade NATs," submitted November 2023** — which does test cross-connectivity across carriers on 5G networks, but the abstract/excerpt fetched did **not** yield an extractable numeric symmetric-NAT percentage (would require a full-PDF read not completed in this pass).
- **Verdict: mark the underlying prevalence number UNKNOWN at HIGH-recency confidence.** The old IMC 2016 figures (~40% symmetric on cellular) are directionally consistent with the "15–30%" range used in REQUIREMENTS.md and are the best citable anchor available, but they should be labeled **"historical measurement, not reverified for 2026"** rather than presented as a current fact. This does not change the practical recommendation already in REQUIREMENTS.md (build relay-fallback first; treat hole-punching as an optimization) — if anything, a decade-old, non-trivial symmetric-NAT rate is still a reason to keep relay-fallback mandatory, not optional — but the specific percentage should not be quoted as current-year data in the Phase 13 decision record.
